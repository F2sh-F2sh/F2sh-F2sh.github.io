The Sandevistan

A wearable, illuminated model of David Martinez’s Sandevistan from Cyberpunk: Edgerunners, designed and 3D-printed from scratch.

Project type
Electrical, mechanical, and artistic
Date completed
September 6
Time spent
Approximately two months
Final cost
$75 USD

Sandevistan Overview

I designed and built a model of the Sandevistan used by the main character, David Martinez, in Cyberpunk: Edgerunners. I started this project because I loved the show and its focus on technology. Combined with my own interest in technology, it felt like the perfect thing to make.

Goal

My goal was to create a spine—the Sandevistan—that could:

  • Be securely attached to my body and worn.
  • Light up.
  • Accurately resemble the Sandevistan from the show.

I also wanted to learn more about 3D modeling, circuitry, and 3D printing throughout the process.

Design and Planning

Before building, I researched similar projects to see what others had done well and which ideas I liked. This helped me develop a clearer vision for my own design. I then created rough 3D models in Blender and Onshape, calculated the necessary dimensions, and planned how the circuitry would fit inside the parts.

I chose CosPLA filament because it met my specific design needs. Blender and Onshape let me create the complex models the project required, and I chose a simple lighting circuit to keep the electronics manageable.

Build Process

  1. Researched and planned the build, identifying all the supplies I needed to buy.
  2. Designed the entire spine from scratch in 3D, using my research as a guide.
  3. Printed the parts and checked them for design flaws.
  4. Tested how the parts fit together and assembled the spine.
  5. Checked that the wiring was secure and could withstand handling.

My main tools and materials were Onshape, Blender, the 3D printers at Wake Tech Community College, LED light strips, Velcro, and a battery pack.

Challenges and Problem-Solving

One of the biggest challenges was that my initial model did not fit the lighting circuit properly. At first, I tried to make the circuit fit, but I eventually realized the design needed to change.

I remeasured the lighting circuit and adjusted the fit and clearance of the spine parts in my model. After resizing and checking the openings, the wires could pass through them properly.

Another challenge was my own perfectionism. I spent weeks making tiny adjustments, which slowed down the overall design process. I overcame this by accepting that the model did not need to be perfect for the project to be worth finishing.

Final Result

The finished project met all my original goals: it could be worn, it lit up, and it resembled the Sandevistan from the show.

The final cost was $75 USD, and the total development time was approximately two months.

What I Learned

Through this project, I became more effective at 3D modeling and gained a better understanding of circuitry and engineering design.

More importantly, I learned that perfectionism can get in the way of progress. If I had kept trying to make the model perfect, I might never have finished the project.

If I built another version, I would improve my workflow by staying more organized and planning exactly which parts I needed to print and where I would print them.
